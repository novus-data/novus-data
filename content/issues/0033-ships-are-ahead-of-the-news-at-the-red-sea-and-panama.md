Sources: IMF PortWatch, GDELT; scores calculated by Novus Data.</p></span><h2>What happened</h2><p>Two of the five chokepoints the Mancalë Index has news data for read "Ships ahead of news" in the week of 2 November 2026: the Red Sea and the Panama Canal. Both held that reading the week before, according to the index's weekly history for the Red Sea and for Panama.</p><p>At the Red Sea (Bab el-Mandeb), ship transits were 54% below the 2019 to 2022 normal while English-language news coverage was 6.7 times normal. The gap between the two scores was −2.37. At Panama, transits were 16% below normal while coverage was 0.2 times normal, a gap of −2.21. A gap of −2 or lower earns the label.</p><p>For contrast, the Strait of Hormuz had transits 95% below normal and coverage 103 times normal. Its gap was −0.80, so it reads "Aligned": the news and the ships tell the same story. Suez, at −1.66, and the Taiwan Strait, at −1.08, are also aligned.</p><h2>Why it matters</h2><p>The index scores two things from 0 to 6. Flow stress measures how unusually few ships passed through, and attention measures how unusually much the world's English-language news mentioned the place. The gap is attention minus flow stress. At the Red Sea, flow stress was 5.3 and attention 2.9. At Panama, flow stress was 2.2 and attention 0.0. So in both places the ships are more disrupted than the coverage would lead a reader to expect.</p><p>The two cases are different in a way the numbers hide. At Panama, the cause is visible in the canal authority's own notices. It set daily booking slots at 33 and the maximum Neopanamax draft at 49 feet in Advisory A-36-2026, and said in Advisory A-39-2026 that the water deficit persists and that vessels without a reservation may face very long delays. A rule-driven squeeze like that rarely makes headlines. My reading is that this is why attention is zero while traffic is still down (estimated). At the Red Sea, the index does not say why traffic is low, and I am not going to guess beyond what the data supports.</p><p>Two cautions come with the index. It describes the past week and does not forecast. And ship counts come from IMF PortWatch, which counts ships from their location signals. Ships in conflict zones may switch those signals off, so counted transits can overstate how far real traffic has fallen.</p><p>One more thing explains why you may see different numbers elsewhere. The index measures against a fixed 2019 to 2022 normal. Other comparisons, including some in the briefing, use the 2025 average, so the same week can look quite different depending on the baseline.</p><h2>How well do these labels work</h2><p>The index has a scorecard that tests its own labels. After a "Ships ahead of news" week, the test asks whether attention rose by at least 1 point in any of the next four weeks, meaning the news caught up with the ships. In the history, found after the fact, that happened in 36 of 70 calls (51%, with a range of 40% to 63%), against 33% from an ordinary week. Those are in-sample results, because the index's thresholds were chosen while looking at the same history.</p><p>The forward record is the real test, and it is not yet settled. Labels have been written to the ledger as they happened since the week of 12 October, and none of the recorded calls has had its four outcome weeks yet.</p><h2>Who it reaches</h2><p>Operators and shippers using the Panama Canal (reported). The canal authority's advisories set the slot and draft limits above and say vessels without a booking may face very long delays. How much that costs any one operator depends on whether it has a booking, which I cannot see. Sources: Advisory A-36-2026 and Advisory A-39-2026, Panama Canal Authority.</p><p>Container lines and shippers on Asia to Europe (reported). Drewry said on 12 November that 53 vessels transited the Suez Canal the previous week, that carriers have announced higher surcharges on Asia to Europe routes and it expects rates there to increase slightly in the coming weeks, and that geopolitical uncertainty remains a risk to container shipping. I cannot say from the index how many of those ships avoid the Red Sea. Source: Drewry World Container Index, 12 November 2026.</p><p>I am not naming individual companies. The index scores places, not firms.</p><h2>What would change this view</h2><ol><li><p>The next refresh. IMF PortWatch publishes new ship data on Tuesdays. If the week of 9 November puts either gap back inside −2 to +2, the "ships ahead of news" reading ends.</p></li><li><p>The scorecard. The first recorded calls will be marked as followed or not once their four weeks pass. A run of misses would weaken how much weight the label deserves.</p></li><li><p>The canal authority's notices. A new advisory from the Panama Canal Authority that cuts or adds slots would change Panama's flow stress directly.</p></li><li><p>News coverage itself. If attention rises at the Red Sea or Panama without traffic changing, the gap narrows because the news caught up, not because the ships recovered. The index cannot tell those apart, but the two scores shown separately can.</p></li></ol><h2>Sources</h2><ol><li><p>Mancalë Index, week of 2 November 2026.
