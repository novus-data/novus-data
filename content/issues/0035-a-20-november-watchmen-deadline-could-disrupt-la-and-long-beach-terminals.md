Photo: Charles Csavossy / U.S. Customs and Border Protection, via Wikimedia Commons, public domain.</p></span><h2>What happened</h2><p>The Pacific Maritime Association, the employers' group at the Los Angeles and Long Beach ports, and ILWU Local 26 <a href="https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/west/2026/11/09/891903.htm?utm_source=alexs-newsletter-408c44.beehiiv.com&amp;utm_medium=newsletter&amp;utm_campaign=a-20-november-watchmen-deadline-could-disrupt-la-and-long-beach-terminals" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">extended their bargaining deadline</a> through 20 November 2026, according to Reuters on 9 November. Local 26 represents about 250 maritime watchmen, roughly 2% of the workers at the busiest container port complex in the US. Watchmen control and inspect truck and employee entries at terminal gates, provide terminal security, and guard and track critical cargo.</p><p>This is a separate contract from the main longshore agreement. FreightWaves reported on 9 November that the union had authorized a strike and first set a deadline of 1 November, which was then moved to 4 November, to 6 November and now to 20 November. The watchmen's previous agreement expired in July 2025, and they have kept working under extensions. The longshore contract, which covers more than 22,000 workers at 29 West Coast ports, runs through July 2028.</p><p>I could not find a statement from the employers' group or Local 26 themselves, and the reports do not say what is in dispute. Everything about the talks below comes from Reuters and FreightWaves.</p><h2>Why it matters</h2><p>Start with the size of the exposure. The Pacific Maritime Association says a strike would disrupt terminals handling roughly 85% of cargo at Long Beach and 33% at Los Angeles (reported). A group that is 2% of the workforce sits at the gates of terminals that handle most of one port's cargo.</p><p>Next, the mechanism. Watchmen staff the point where trucks and people enter a terminal. If they stop work, gate processing and security at those terminals can slow or stop, and containers wait longer to leave or enter (inferred). The reports do not say how a stoppage would work in practice, so how much cargo would be delayed, and for how long, is not something I can estimate.</p><p>Then the timing against volumes. The National Retail Federation and Hackett Associates said on 9 November that September imports at the ports they track were 2.37 million twenty-foot container units, known as TEU, up 12.6% from a year earlier and the busiest month of 2026. They estimate October at 2.26 million TEU and forecast November at 2.05 million, which is about 9% below October by my arithmetic. Jonathan Gold, the federation's vice president for supply chain and customs policy, said high volumes have caused cargo backups at some ports, particularly on the West Coast, and that "most holiday merchandise is already here."</p><p>My reading is that this lowers the risk to holiday shelves but not to cargo that is already waiting, or to the replenishment orders that follow the holidays (estimated). The federation's own forecast has January 2027 imports 3% below a year earlier, so the pressure on volumes eases after the new year.</p><h2>Who it reaches</h2><p><b>Importers with containers waiting at Long Beach and Los Angeles terminals (inferred).</b> A gate or security stoppage delays cargo that is already at the port. The employers' figures say which port is more exposed: terminals handling about 85% of Long Beach cargo against 33% at Los Angeles. I cannot see which importers use which terminals, so I am not naming any. Sources: Reuters, 9 November 2026; the employers' group's figures as reported there.</p><p><b>Retailers restocking after the holidays (estimated).</b> The federation says most holiday merchandise has arrived, so the holiday season itself is less exposed. A stoppage that outlasts the holidays would reach replenishment. That is a judgement from the federation's forecast and the employers' terminal shares, not a reported effect. Source: National Retail Federation and Hackett Associates, Global Port Tracker, 9 November 2026.</p><p><b>Trucking and port-adjacent operators who work through terminal gates (inferred).</b> Watchmen control entries, so truck access is the first thing a stoppage would touch. The reports do not describe truck or rail effects, so I cannot say how large they would be. Source: Reuters, 9 November 2026.</p><h2>What would change this view</h2><ol><li><p><b>A deal or another extension on or before 20 November.</b> Either would end the risk for now, or defer it. Watch Reuters and statements from the employers' group. A new deadline would show the talks still unresolved.</p></li><li><p><b>Strike notice or a walkout.</b> The union has authorized a strike, so a notice would turn a risk into a date. Reports on terminal operations would show which terminals are affected.</p></li><li><p><b>Cargo counts for October and November.</b> Each port publishes monthly cargo statistics, and a drop after 20 November would be the first hard sign of an effect. The federation's forecast for November, 2.05 million TEU, is the number to compare against.</p></li><li><p><b>The next Global Port Tracker.</b> The federation updates it monthly. If it moves its forecast for December or January 2027 after the deadline passes, that is the federation's view of an effect.</p></li></ol><h2>Sources</h2><ol><li><p>Import Cargo Still Strong as Peak Season Winds Down.
